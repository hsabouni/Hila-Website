Finding the Right Sensory Cue for VR Navigation

Research question: Which sensory cue helps people find a target most effectively and efficiently in a virtual museum?

One-way ANOVA with Tukey post-hoc; both effects large (ω² = 0.31 and 0.44).

My role Researcher and experience designer

I led this study end to end as the researcher and experience designer: framing the question, building the VR environment and cue conditions, recruiting and testing 17 participants per condition, and analyzing the behavioral and qualitative data.

Project context Controlled VR study, four cue conditions

A mixed-method, between-subjects experiment, chosen to avoid carryover and limit headset time, with 17 participants per condition using behavioral measures, validated questionnaires, and qualitative responses.

Key result Haptic guidance emerged strongest overall

Active cues reduced task-list checks, while the mirror increased checks and completion time. All audio and haptic participants found the cues beneficial; workload, presence, and sickness showed no significant differences.

The rear-view mirror condition expanded visibility behind the participant but also introduced competing visual information.

Method

01Build

Recreated part of the Louvre in Rhinoceros 3D and Unity, placed seven target paintings, and built the Meta Quest Pro interactions.

02Study design

Designed a controlled between-subjects comparison that isolated four guidance conditions while keeping the museum task consistent.

03Data collection

Recruited and tested 71 participants using a consistent screening, instruction, practice, task, and survey protocol.

04Analysis

Combined task behavior, completion time, validated questionnaires, and qualitative responses to explain both performance and experience.

Study conditions

Four sensory guidance conditions were tested in the same museum task.

ControlNo guidance

A 90-degree forward field of view with no additional cue.

MirrorVisual guidance

A rear-view display extended awareness behind the participant.

AudioProximity guidance

A beep activated within five meters of a target.

HapticProximity guidance

Controller vibration activated within five meters of a target.

task design

  • Task

    The task was an engaging scavenger hunt in a museum, where participants were asked to find seven specific paintings among the many on display.

  • spatial environment

    Google map, AutoCad, Rhinoceros 3D

    The virtual environment recreated a section of the Louvre Museum's Denon wing, featuring rooms 700–702 and 710–712 to ensure a balanced layout. Approximately 30% of the paintings were removed to maintain participant focus.

  • 3D modeling

    Rhinoceros 3D

    Rooms were modeled in Rhinoceros 3D, highlighting key architectural elements while omitting intricate details to avoid distracting participants.

  • Equipment, Interactions

    Unity

    The model was imported into Unity, where seven target paintings were placed across rooms. Participants used a Meta Quest Pro with a counter showing collected paintings and a task list to aid memory.

  • Conditions

    Unity

    Four sensory cue conditions were tested: 1)Control Condition: 90° front field of view, no cues, 2)Mirror Condition: Rear-view mirror extending vision behind participants, 3)Audio Condition: Beep sound within a 5-meter radius of targets, 4)Haptic Condition: Controller vibration within a 5-meter radius of targets. Each participant experienced only one condition, maintaining task consistency across groups.

study design

  • Participants

    Qualtrics

    The study included 71 university participants (30 female, 41 male), aged 18-35, screened for eligibility based on vision and motion sensitivity. Participants varied in gaming experience, with only two having prior, minimal familiarity with the Louvre.

  • Setting

    Experiments were conducted in a distraction-free lab space, where participants navigated the virtual environment seated, using controllers for movement and interaction.

  • Study Procedure

    Unity

    Participants reviewed the seven target paintings, completed a three-minute controller practice, and then entered the timed 20-minute museum task. The target list remained available in VR so the study measured navigation and cue effectiveness rather than memory.

  • Survey

    After completing the task, participants filled out a survey on task enjoyment, immersion, cue effectiveness, and willingness to try similar tasks, followed by the NASA-TLX, Presence Questionnaire, and Simulator Sickness Questionnaire.

Findings

Audio and haptic cues helped participants orient toward targets with fewer task-list checks. The rear-view mirror produced the opposite pattern: participants checked the list more often and took longer to finish. The added visual channel appears to have competed for attention rather than reducing search effort.

01Active cues reduced search burden

Audio and haptic guidance helped participants orient toward targets with fewer task-list checks.

02More visual information was not better

The mirror condition increased checks and completion time, suggesting additional input raised cognitive load.

03Efficiency and experience aligned

Participants described audio and haptic cues as helpful and engaging, supporting the behavioral results.

What to notice: active cues reduced search effort, while the mirror condition increased checking behavior and completion time.

What to notice: participants connected active cues with effectiveness and engagement, while the mirror was used infrequently and was more often described as challenging.

Qualitative Findings and Analysis

Survey responses were grouped into four categories—effective, challenging, engaging, and time awareness—and distilled into two key themes: effectiveness and engagement.
Effectiveness: Participants in the audio and haptic conditions consistently praised the cues, noting they helped prevent missed paintings. In contrast, about half of those in the mirror condition rarely used the mirror, with only 6% relying on it frequently. Many participants in the control and mirror conditions described the task as challenging, while those in the audio and haptic conditions found it more manageable.
Engagement: Guidance cues boosted engagement across all groups. Enjoyment was highest in the audio (100%) and haptic (86%) conditions, where cues helped participants stay focused. Time perception varied, with most participants in the audio (76%) and control (70%) conditions experiencing a "loss of time," reflecting deep engagement in the task.

Qualitative responses converged around effectiveness, challenge, engagement, and time awareness.

Product and Design Impact

Final design implication

Prioritize haptic guidance for comparable VR search tasks; avoid persistent visual cues unless the task requires continuous rear awareness.

The study did not lead to a shipped product change. Its design recommendation is to validate proximity-based vibration that increases near a target, offers distinct directional patterns, and lets users adjust intensity or switch to an audio fallback. This direction follows the evidence that haptics supported orientation without occupying the visual channel, while the rear-view display increased visual competition.

Designing a cue means designing attention

A feature can provide more information and still make performance worse. Every cue must be evaluated by what it helps people ignore as well as what it helps them notice.

Efficiency needs multiple measures

Completion time alone would not explain the experience. Combining time, task-list checks, workload, presence, sickness, and participant accounts revealed why each condition performed differently.

Protocol consistency is part of research quality

Testing 71 people in VR required consistent instruction, safety screening, equipment setup, practice, and observation. Small procedural differences could otherwise become confounds.

What I would change next

I would measure path efficiency, head movement, cue-response latency, and missed-target recovery in addition to completion time. I would also prototype alternative mirror placements before the main study, test adjustable cue intensity and accessibility preferences, and use a counterbalanced within-subject follow-up to separate cue effectiveness from individual differences and novelty effects.

Among all the things I learned during this project, one of the most transformative was honing my time management skills. I balanced a wide range of tasks—from 3D modeling to game interaction development, literature review, and pilot tests—while continuously adapting my workflow to meet shifting priorities.

Along the way, I gained valuable insights into working with human subjects, particularly in the intricate world of VR, where precision, attention to detail, and clear eligibility criteria are essential to ensure safety and reliable outcome. Perhaps the most challenging and rewarding lesson was cultivating patience and consistency; recruiting and testing 71 participants, with each session taking about an hour, was demanding. Remaining as attentive and consistent with each participant, from the first to the last, required endurance, as the study spanned two months of daily sessions. This journey not only strengthened my skills but also deepened my passion for innovative, human-centered design and research.
