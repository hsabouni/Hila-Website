Using a Confederate in a Group Decision Study

I study how a teammate’s agreement or disagreement changes the subjective decisions people make with GenAI. Unlike an individual user study, this work introduces a social layer: each participant must interpret both an AI recommendation and another person’s judgment.

That social layer creates a challenge for experimental control. Each partner introduces interpersonal variation that can shape the discussion in directions unrelated to the factor I want to examine. Pairing two independently recruited participants would make the exchange natural, but it would also make each session difficult to compare.

My solution was to keep two people in the interaction while making one role controlled. One person is the participant. The other is a confederate, a trained research-team member who appears to be another participant. She knows the study protocol, remains natural and neutral, and follows scripts prepared for every decision the participant might make.

Designing that role became one of the most important and difficult parts of the study. This note focuses on why the confederate was necessary, how I designed her participation, and what this method made possible and complicated.

Why not pair two participants?

Pairing participants with one another would produce more naturally varied conversations. It would also introduce many differences that I could not control.

One partner might be highly confident, while another might sound uncertain. Someone might introduce a strong new argument, draw on personal experience, speak at length, or actively push for consensus. Any of these behaviors could change the other participant’s decision.

Those differences are meaningful in real collaboration. But for this stage of the research, they would make it difficult to answer a narrower question: can another person’s agreement or disagreement affect someone’s opinion even when that person provides no new evidence?

Using a confederate allows me to vary the social position while keeping the rest of the interaction as comparable as possible.

Creating decisions without a correct answer

The task places participants in the role of a startup team preparing for an investor demonstration. Across eight scenarios, they make decisions about areas such as feature prioritization and pricing strategy.

I intentionally designed the scenarios to be subjective. Neither option is objectively correct, and participants cannot resolve the disagreement by finding a factual error.

This distinction matters. If a participant changes their mind after learning that their original answer was incorrect, that is informational influence. I am interested in something more difficult to isolate: whether another person’s judgment can affect how someone interprets the same GenAI recommendation and evaluates their own position.

Participants see ChatGPT’s recommendation as a static screenshot rather than interacting with the model directly. This ensures that everyone receives the same information, in the same amount, and with the same presentation. It also removes the variability that a live conversation with GenAI would introduce.

Designing the confederate’s role

The confederate follows a predetermined agreement or disagreement condition for each scenario.

After participants complete the individual phase, the moderator privately shares their initial decisions with the confederate. The confederate can then express either the same position or the opposing position, depending on the assigned condition.

Prepared responses exist for both available options. This means the confederate can follow the assigned condition regardless of what the participant initially selects.

The order of the scenarios, who speaks first, and which scenarios involve agreement or disagreement are counterbalanced across sessions. This helps prevent the results from depending on a particular item or interaction order.

The hardest part: control without sounding artificial

Creating the script was more difficult than simply writing neutral responses.

If the confederate explained too much, she could introduce new evidence or become more persuasive in some scenarios than others. If every response was extremely short, however, the interaction felt unnatural. Participants could interpret her as shy, disengaged, reluctant, or uncertain. Those impressions could become another source of influence.

I worked through this problem across ten pilot sessions.

The resulting protocol uses three conversational turns from each person. The confederate’s first turn states her position briefly. The second restates the same point in a slightly longer form. The third rephrases that position again without adding a new argument.

The goal is not to make every conversation identical. That would be impossible, and probably undesirable. The goal is to keep the confederate’s informational contribution stable enough that agreement or disagreement remains the main difference between conditions.

Neutrality involves more than words

A controlled script does not automatically produce a controlled interaction.

Tone of voice, facial expression, posture, hesitation, and enthusiasm can all communicate confidence or pressure. The confederate is therefore trained to maintain a consistent and neutral presentation throughout each session.

She also completes the same visible study activities as the participant: joining the meeting, introducing herself, participating in the warm-up activity, moving through both phases, and appearing to complete the private surveys.

The warm-up is especially useful. Before beginning the decision task, both people introduce themselves, share a fun fact, and list five things they would bring on a trip to Mars. This helps them become comfortable with the shared Figma workspace and establishes the turn-taking structure they will use later.

It also gives the interaction a more natural beginning than immediately placing two apparent strangers into a structured disagreement.

What this design allows me to examine

Participants privately answer the same questions before and after each discussion:

  • Do they agree or disagree with ChatGPT’s recommendation?
  • How confident are they in that judgment?
  • How likely are they to seek additional information before acting?

When someone changes their opinion, they also explain what motivated the change.

Together, these measures let me look beyond whether a final answer changed. A participant might retain the same decision while becoming less confident. They might become more interested in verifying the recommendation. Alternatively, they might change their answer without becoming more confident in it.

These changes can reveal forms of social influence that would disappear if I measured only the final decision.

The methodological trade-off

Using a confederate gives the study consistency, but that consistency comes with limitations.

The interaction cannot capture the full complexity of a naturally formed team. Real collaborators have histories, roles, expertise, status differences, and existing relationships. They can introduce evidence, challenge assumptions, misunderstand one another, or shift the direction of a conversation unexpectedly.

This study deliberately reduces that complexity. It is not intended to reproduce every aspect of workplace collaboration. Instead, it creates a controlled starting point for examining how peer judgment enters a person’s relationship with GenAI.

Subsequent research can reintroduce more of the variability found in real teams. This study establishes a controlled starting point for understanding the influence of another person’s position.

What I learned from the process

Designing this protocol has reinforced something that can be easy to overlook when studying human–AI interaction: another person’s judgment is not simply background context.

It can affect how people interpret the GenAI recommendation, evaluate their own reasoning, decide whether more information is needed, and determine how confidently they move forward.

The confederate is a methodological tool, but designing her role has also become part of the research itself. Every decision about what she says, how she says it, and what she deliberately leaves out reflects a larger question: how can we study social influence without removing the social qualities that make an interaction believable?

The balance between experimental control and natural interaction is inherently imperfect. That tension is exactly what makes this method valuable and worth documenting as part of the research process.
