How People Make Consequential Decisions with AI Under Social Influence

PhD Research · Human–AI Interaction · Ongoing

I study how people evaluate generative AI recommendations when decisions are subjective, consequential, and made with others. My research asks what people trust, when they seek verification, and how a teammate’s position changes individual and collective judgment.

My role: I lead the research from question formulation and study design through data collection, analysis, and communication.

The Research Problem

Most research on decision-making with generative AI examines a person interacting with AI alone. In practice, AI outputs are brought into meetings, discussed among teammates, and evaluated alongside other people’s judgments. That introduces a second source of uncertainty: people must interpret not only the AI recommendation, but also one another.

A teammate’s agreement may reinforce an unverified recommendation. Disagreement may encourage closer scrutiny—or cause someone to abandon a sound judgment. Understanding these dynamics is necessary for designing AI-assisted workflows that support independent thinking without ignoring the value of collaboration.

A Research Program in Two Phases

The first study revealed a difference in verification expectations. The second tests what happens when judgments meet.

01

Foundational study

Disciplinary Differences in AI Verification

Do engineers and designers approach the verification of AI-generated outputs differently?

A mixed-method survey of 117 engineering and design students examined AI use, confidence, perceived value, trust, and expectations for verification in individual and team settings.

What it established: Both groups used and valued AI similarly, but engineers reported stronger expectations that AI outputs should be verified. Disciplinary training may shape standards of evaluation more than adoption itself.

The question this opened

If people bring different verification expectations into collaboration, whose judgment shapes the group’s response to AI?

02

Main study · Current phase

Social Influence in the Evaluation of Generative AI Outputs

How does a teammate’s agreement or disagreement affect the way people evaluate an AI recommendation?

In a controlled within-subject experiment, graduate students first evaluate ChatGPT recommendations independently, then discuss the same subjective business decisions with a teammate following a standardized agreement or disagreement script.

What it measures: Changes in decisions, confidence, and intention to seek additional information. The design isolates the social effect of a teammate’s stance without adding new evidence to the discussion.

Data collection and analysis are ongoing. Findings will be added when the evidence is ready.

Why This Design Matters

Agreement and disagreement usually arrive with arguments, evidence, tone, and interpersonal pressure. The main study deliberately separates a teammate’s position from those additional signals.

A trained confederate follows a pilot-tested, behaviorally neutral script. Participants provide private responses before and after discussion, allowing each person to serve as their own baseline. This makes it possible to examine whether the social position itself changes judgment, confidence, or verification behavior.

Design Opportunity

This work can inform collaborative AI systems that protect independent judgment while supporting productive deliberation.

Possible interventions include collecting private evaluations before group discussion, making uncertainty visible without turning it into pressure, and prompting teams to distinguish social agreement from evidence-based verification.
